**In 2026, the Cochrane systematic review submission process is: submit a Review Proposal → once approved, write and submit the Protocol → peer review and revision → Protocol publication → complete the Full Review within one year.** For our team, it took about 8 months from the first email to the protocol being published.

I worked on this protocol as first author during my sixth year of medical school, and I started as a PGY physician in August 2026. That same month, "[Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) for chronic neck pain](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD016383/full)" (CD016383) was published in the Cochrane Library. It is also listed on [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42635100/).

There are few first-hand accounts of submitting to Cochrane in Chinese. The two posts by Dr. Liang-Tseng Kuo (郭亮增) ([Q&A](https://ymrf2015.innovarad.tw/qa_ltkuo/), [blog](https://drkuolt.com/blog/3056b261-ddca-4da2-bea9-eb268ae359ac), both in Chinese) are well worth reading first. Cochrane has changed its submission process in the last few years, though, so this post records what we actually went through under the new process, for medical students and early-career doctors thinking about a Cochrane review.

## Key points

- **The first step is now called a Review Proposal, not Title Registration.** It is handled centrally by the Cochrane Central Editorial Service through Editorial Manager.
- **To update an existing review, you must contact *all* of the original authors**, not just the corresponding author.
- **Protocols are limited to 6,000 words and reviews to 12,000 words** (abstract and main text, excluding references and supplementary material), enforced from July 2026.
- **The MEDLINE search strategy must involve an Information Specialist or medical librarian**, and Cochrane's official matching service is paid.
- **Protocol publication is free.** For immediate open access to the full review (Gold OA), the APC is US$5,100.
- **AI use must be disclosed.**

## Glossary

Newcomers to Cochrane run into a lot of acronyms, so here they are up front:

- **Proposal**: the topic proposal, explaining why the question matters and who is on the team and what each person does. You can only write the protocol once it is approved.
- **Protocol**: the study plan, written in advance with the eligibility criteria, search strategy and analysis methods. It is published after peer review so the methods cannot be changed after the fact.
- **RevMan Web**: Cochrane's online authoring platform, used for both protocols and reviews.
- **Editorial Manager**: the submission and peer review system.
- **Information Specialist (IS)**: a trained specialist, usually a medical librarian, who designs the literature search strategy.
- **DOI form (Declaration of Interest)**: the conflict of interest declaration every author must complete.
- **RoB 2**: Cochrane's tool for assessing risk of bias in randomised controlled trials.
- **GRADE / SoF table**: the method for rating the certainty of evidence, with results summarised in a Summary of Findings table.

## How is the new process different from the old one?

Dr. Kuo's posts describe three stages: Title registration → Protocol → Full review. The overall shape is the same, but the first step is now a **Review Proposal**: a form in Editorial Manager where you explain why the question matters, how it relates to existing reviews, who is on the team and what each person does, and whether you have Information Specialist support.

Also, submissions used to be handled by each Review Group separately; now the **Central Editorial Service** handles submissions and peer review centrally. Our topic was an update of a 2019 review (CD011927). We ended up starting over with a new protocol, so it received a new number, CD016383.

## What happens at the proposal stage?

### 1. To update someone else's review, contact all of the original authors first

Under Cochrane's authorship policy, before updating an existing review you need to ask the original authors whether they want to join, or whether they agree to a new team taking it over. We emailed the original corresponding author, gave about two weeks to reply, and explained that if we heard nothing, we would attach the correspondence to the proposal.

The original corresponding author never replied. After the proposal was approved, Cochrane Support wrote to say that **contacting only the corresponding author is not enough; all original authors must be contacted.** They offered to send the emails on our behalf with a 30-day deadline. This step cost us another month, but it is also how one of the original review authors came to join us. She has extensive experience in systematic review methods and later helped a great deal with revising the protocol and the search strategy.

> **Tip:** Put **all** of the original authors on your very first email. It saves a whole round of waiting.

### 2. Decide early who will handle the search strategy

The proposal form asks whether you have Information Specialist support. We only found out later that **Cochrane's official IS matching is a paid service.** When we asked, Cochrane Support referred us to the relevant Review Group and explained that we could also pay a freelance IS ourselves, though we are still waiting for a detailed quote.

> **Tip:** Ideally, have someone on the team who knows how to search from the start, or check early whether your hospital librarian can help. If no one on the team can, you can ask Cochrane to match you with an IS after the protocol is approved.

### 3. Who should be on the team?

We ended up with five authors:

- **First author (me)**: drafted the protocol, worked in RevMan, and handled the admin and correspondence
- **A PGY physician**: shares the RoB assessment and GRADE with me
- **A professor from Cochrane Taiwan**: has Cochrane review experience and oversees the methods
- **An author of the original review**: reviews the methods and arbitrates when we disagree on RoB or GRADE
- **Corresponding author (main supervisor)**: coordinates the project and provides clinical expertise

The team needs a mix of methods, statistics and clinical expertise. In particular, having a senior author who has done a Cochrane review before helps you avoid many common beginner mistakes.

## What do you need to write and submit the protocol?

Once the proposal is approved you receive a formal invitation, and the protocol is set up in **RevMan Web**. Every author needs a Cochrane account. You get about three months from invitation to deadline; we had started the first draft while waiting for the original authors to reply, so we submitted within two weeks.

Before submitting, prepare:

- **The pre-submission checklist** and author checklist
- **DOI forms from all authors**: the corresponding author collects them, writes a summary in RevMan and uploads them together
- **The [preliminary search strategy](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD016383/supplementarymaterials/CD016383-SUP-01-searchStrategy.html)**
- **The corresponding author's ORCID**

Pitfalls we ran into:

- **Mismatched author account emails.** The invitation went to one email address, but a co-author's existing Cochrane account was registered under another, so they could not see the protocol in RevMan. We had to email Cochrane Support to fix it.
- **Only the corresponding author can submit in Editorial Manager**; other authors cannot submit on their behalf.
- **Authors must accept the invitation first** before they appear on the author list in RevMan.
- **The DOI forms must match what is written in RevMan.** The editor pointed out that one form was blank while RevMan had content, and we had to complete it again.
- **State the study designs clearly.** For example, whether cluster-RCTs are included, and if so, which risk of bias tool you will use for them.

## What do Cochrane peer reviewers ask about?

Our protocol was reviewed by six roles: the sign-off editor (final decision), a methods reviewer, an Information Specialist, a clinical expert, a consumer reviewer and the managing editor. The decision was minor revision, **with only two weeks to respond**: revise the content in RevMan, write a point-by-point response and upload it to Editorial Manager.

Below are four kinds of comments. All of them are problems you can avoid in your next protocol simply by knowing about them in advance:

### 1. You must say how you will identify problematic studies

**Comment:** The editor noted that this field is dominated by small trials with a high risk of including problematic studies, and asked us to describe how we would assess trial trustworthiness. The Information Specialist also asked how we would track post-publication corrections and retractions.

**Why it matters:** This reflects Cochrane's recent policy on problematic studies, which older protocol templates usually do not cover.

**What we changed:** We added the **INSPECT-SR** tool to screen all potentially eligible studies, and we check **Retraction Watch** and PubMed for retractions, expressions of concern and errata.

### 2. Statistical methods must name the specific estimators

**Comment:** Writing "we will use a random-effects model" is not enough. You need to state which method you will use to estimate the between-study heterogeneity variance and which method you will use to calculate confidence intervals.

**What we changed:** We use **REML** to estimate heterogeneity. When there are at least 3 studies and heterogeneity is greater than 0, we use **Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ)** confidence intervals; otherwise we use Wald-type intervals. The reviewer provided example wording, so we simply followed it.

Sensitivity analyses also need to be stated precisely: say whether you will do "one" or "two", rather than glossing over it with "or".

### 3. Define the primary timepoint and Summary of Findings tables in advance

**Comment:** We needed to state the primary timepoint, which timepoint the risk of bias assessment applies to, and which SoF tables we would produce. A SoF table can include at most the 7 most important outcomes, and the order of the outcomes section must match the SoF table.

**Why it matters:** Defining these in advance prevents picking a "nicer-looking" timepoint after seeing the data.

**What we changed:** We set the primary timepoint as short term (more than 1 day up to 3 months) and listed three SoF tables: TENS versus inactive control, versus other active treatments, and as an add-on treatment. We also reordered the important outcomes.

### 4. Patient involvement needs more than citing one consensus statement

**Comment:** The consumer reviewer felt that citing the IMMPACT consensus alone was not enough to justify the choice of outcomes, and wanted more direct patient involvement.

**What we changed:** We committed to inviting 1–2 people with chronic neck pain to review the Plain Language Summary at the full review stage.

### How we handled the word limit

This version of the protocol was not subject to the word limit, but the editor reminded us that the full review will have a 12,000-word cap. So while adding the material the reviewers asked for, we also trimmed part of the imaging and treatment paragraphs in the background.

## Can you use AI for a Cochrane review? How do you disclose it?

**Yes, but you must disclose it.** In the protocol's "Disclosure of artificial intelligence use" section, we stated that we used **Gemini 3.1 Pro to help with language editing and stylistic polishing**, and that the authors take full responsibility for the content.

The editorial team's decision letter also said they encourage responsible use of AI tools to tighten the text and reduce repetition, but all content must be accurate and disclosed according to Cochrane policy. In addition, **if AI is used at the full review stage for translation, study screening, data extraction, risk of bias assessment or GRADE, you must name the tool and its purpose in the corresponding methods section**, not just in the closing declaration.

AI is good for polishing prose, but it cannot replace checking the content: no matter who wrote the text, verify every citation yourself. I will write separately about how I use AI tools for clinical learning and for organising the literature.

## What happens after the protocol is accepted?

- **Copy editing**: you receive the copy editor's comments within about a week, and have about a week to make the corrections in RevMan.
- **Confirm author affiliations**: they cannot be changed after publication, and the author order cannot change at this stage either.
- **Licence for Publication**: every author completes their own, each with a different link.
- **Add required content**: the editor asked us to state the primary timepoint in the "Certainty of the evidence assessment" section too, even though it was already stated elsewhere.
- **Get consent for acknowledgements**: authors must obtain written consent from anyone acknowledged in the review. The editorial team does not need to see proof, but the authors are responsible for it.

The **365-day deadline for the full review** starts on the day of publication. Also, the formal literature search must be run within 12 months of the expected publication date (MECIR C37, C38), so plan the search date backwards from there.

## How much does it cost to publish with Cochrane?

| Item | Cost |
| --- | --- |
| Proposal, protocol peer review and publication | Free (protocols are open access under CC BY-NC by default) |
| Full review: Green OA | Free; full text becomes open 12 months after publication |
| Full review: Gold OA | APC **US$5,100**, open immediately on publication; US$2,550 for an update if the original review already paid an APC |
| Information Specialist | Self-funded, whether through official matching or a freelancer |

See the [Cochrane Library website](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/about/open-access) for current prices. If you want Gold OA, look out for research grants from your hospital or foundations; we are applying for one now.

## Pre-submission self-check

- [ ] Updating an existing review: contacted **all** original authors and kept records
- [ ] Decided who handles the search strategy (IS / librarian)
- [ ] All authors have Cochrane accounts, with emails matching the invitation
- [ ] Study designs to be included are stated (including cluster-RCTs and cross-over trials)
- [ ] Method for identifying problematic studies is described (INSPECT-SR, retraction checks)
- [ ] Random-effects analysis names the heterogeneity estimator (REML) and confidence interval method (HKSJ)
- [ ] Primary timepoint, SoF table comparisons and outcome order are all defined
- [ ] Sensitivity analyses state how many and how they will be done
- [ ] Every citation has been checked against the original source
- [ ] Patient or public involvement is described
- [ ] AI use is disclosed
- [ ] DOI forms match RevMan
- [ ] Word count is within the limit, with room left for revisions

## Lessons learned

1. **Start as early as possible.** Waiting for the original authors to reply took more than two months, and that time is best spent drafting the protocol.
2. **Find the right co-authors.** Having an original author join was not just a formality; it made a big difference to the methodological quality.
3. **Include your reference numbers in every email.** Put the manuscript number, Review ID and EMID in, and Cochrane Support will handle it faster.
4. **Peer review comments are a free methods course.** This round of revisions taught me more about statistics and study design than reading several chapters of the Handbook.
5. **Make good use of the free Cochrane Handbook.** The [Cochrane Handbook](https://training.cochrane.org/handbook) is freely available in full. You can load the whole thing into [NotebookLM](https://notebooklm.google.com/) and quickly look up what you need while writing the protocol or responding to reviewers.
